The Pomodoro Technique, explained

Work for 25 minutes, rest for 5, repeat. That is the whole method, and it has helped people concentrate for more than thirty years. This guide covers where it comes from, the exact steps, why it works, and how to adapt it when 25 minutes doesn't fit you.

What it is

The Pomodoro Technique is a way of working in short, timed sessions. You pick one task, set a timer for 25 minutes, and work on nothing else until it rings. Then you take a five-minute break. Each 25-minute session is called a pomodoro. After four of them you take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes.

The timer does two jobs. It makes starting easy, because 25 minutes is a small promise. And it makes stopping mandatory, so you rest before your attention wears out rather than after.

Where it comes from

Francesco Cirillo developed the technique in the late 1980s while he was a university student in Italy. Struggling to concentrate, he bet himself that he could study for just ten minutes, and he kept time with a kitchen timer shaped like a tomato. Pomodoro is the Italian word for tomato. Over years of use he refined the method, settled on 25-minute sessions, and eventually published it in a short guide that grew into the book The Pomodoro Technique.

That wind-up timer is why the timer on this site is drawn as a dial with sixty ticks: you can see the minutes leaving, the way Cirillo could.

The six steps

Cirillo's method is more specific than "work for 25 minutes". These are the steps as he describes them, with the details that people usually miss.

  1. Choose a task. One task, defined clearly enough that you know what progress looks like for this session. "Write the introduction" is a task. "Work on the report" is not.
  2. Set the timer to 25 minutes. Winding the timer is a commitment: for the next 25 minutes, this is the only thing.
  3. Work until it rings. If another thought or errand comes to mind, write it on a list and return to the task. A pomodoro is indivisible. If it is truly interrupted, it does not count, and you start a fresh one.
  4. Mark the pomodoro and take a 5-minute break. Stand up, look away from the screen, get some water. The break is part of the method, not a reward you may skip.
  5. After four pomodoros, take a longer break. Fifteen to thirty minutes. Your attention needs a real reset, not another five minutes.
  6. Record and review. Keep a count of pomodoros per task. Within a few days you learn how many a kind of work really takes, and your estimates become honest.

Why it works

Starting is the hard part

Most procrastination is a problem of starting, not of ability. A 25-minute session is a small enough promise to beat it. You are not agreeing to finish the report, only to work on it until the bell. Once you are in, continuing is far easier than starting was.

Time becomes visible

People reliably underestimate how long work takes, an effect the psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky named the planning fallacy in 1979. Counting pomodoros replaces guesses with a record. After a week you know that "a quick edit" is three pomodoros, and you plan accordingly.

Attention fades with time on task

Performance on a sustained task declines the longer you stay on it, which researchers call the vigilance decrement. In a 2011 study published in the journal Cognition, Atsunori Ariga and Alejandro Lleras had people perform a 40-minute attention task. Those who took brief breaks from it kept their performance steady; those who worked straight through got steadily worse. Short, regular breaks are not lost time. They are what keeps the working minutes productive.

It trains single-tasking

Switching between tasks carries a measurable cost each time you do it, because your mind has to reload the context of what you were doing. A pomodoro is a protected block: one task, no switching, with a rule for what to do when something else shows up. Do that eight times a day and single-tasking becomes a habit instead of an effort.

Breaks with an end are easier to take

A break that could last anywhere from two minutes to an hour is hard to start and harder to end. A five-minute break with a timer is neither. You take it without guilt and come back without negotiation.

How long should a pomodoro be?

Twenty-five and five is the default because it worked for Cirillo and works for most people starting out, not because 25 is a magic number. Common variations:

IntervalSuitsNotes
25 / 5Beginners, mixed work, studyingThe classic. Four rounds, then a break of 15 to 30 minutes.
50 / 10Writing, programming, settled routinesA one-hour rhythm with fewer restarts. Good once starting is no longer your problem.
52 / 17Office workFrom an analysis by the time-tracking company DeskTime of how its most productive users worked. The exact numbers matter less than the ratio.
90 / 20Deep work, research, designLong sessions need a long break and are hard to start. Use them when you know you will get into flow.
15 / 3 or 10 / 2Hard-to-start tasks, low-energy days, ADHDShort sessions get you moving. Lengthen them once the task has momentum.

A practical rule: start with 25. If you regularly hit your stride and resent the bell, move to 50/10. If you struggle to start at all, shorten to 15 or 10 and lengthen once starting feels easy. Change one thing at a time and keep it for a week before judging.

Handling interruptions

Cirillo separates interruptions into two kinds, and each has a routine.

Internal interruptions come from you: a thought about an email, an urge to check something, a sudden idea for another project. Write it down in one line and go back to the task. The urge passes within a minute, and the note is waiting for you at the break. Most of the notes turn out to be unimportant, which is itself a useful discovery.

External interruptions come from other people. Cirillo's answer is a four-step routine he calls inform, negotiate, schedule, call back: tell the person you are in the middle of something, agree on a time, note it, and follow through. "Can I get back to you at three?" works far more often than people expect. Very few requests cannot wait twenty minutes.

When an interruption truly cannot wait, deal with it, void the pomodoro, and start a new one afterwards. Counting a broken session as a whole one only makes your records lie to you.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping breaks because you are "in flow". The point is to rest before you fade. If you constantly want to keep going, lengthen the session instead of dropping the breaks.
  • Taking breaks on a screen. Five minutes of scrolling is not rest. Move, look at something far away, drink water.
  • Treating the count as a score. More pomodoros is not the goal. Finishing the right work is. A day with four well-chosen sessions can beat a day with twelve scattered ones.
  • Several tasks in one session. One task per pomodoro. If a task is smaller than a session, batch small tasks of the same kind into one.
  • Starting with long sessions. Ninety-minute sessions on day one usually end the experiment on day two.
  • Never looking at the record. Estimates only improve when you compare them with what actually happened.

Questions

How many pomodoros a day?
A full day of focused work is roughly 8 to 12 pomodoros. Many people find that 6 to 8 is what they truly get once meetings and life are subtracted, and that is normal. Track for a week before judging yourself.
What if I finish the task early?
Use the remaining minutes to review what you did, plan the next step, or polish it. Cirillo calls this overlearning. The session still ends at the bell.
Can I pause a pomodoro?
By the rules, no: an interrupted pomodoro is void. A timer that punishes you helps nobody, though, so this one lets you pause. Try to reserve it for real interruptions.
Is it good for studying?
Yes. It is where the method was born. Reading, problem sets and revision all fit naturally into 25-minute blocks, and the breaks help memory settle.
Does it help with ADHD?
Many people with ADHD find that a short, externally timed session with a visible countdown helps with starting and with losing track of time. Shorter sessions of 10 to 15 minutes are a common adaptation. This is experience, not medical advice.
Do I need an app?
A kitchen timer works, and it is how the method started. An app adds the counting, the tasks, the stats, and a bell that does not annoy the whole room.
